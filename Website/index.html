Same measurement, two places

Same data source, same method and the same scale, so the only difference is the place.

Trends depend on the place: the same measurement can rise in one place and stay flat, or fall, in another. A trend shows what changed, not why.

Method

How we decide if a change is real

Simple, transparent statistics, the same for every place and measurement. A straight line is a simplification, and we say so.

00

Read the NASA record

Monthly values since 1981 for the grid square (about 50 km wide) around your place, from NASA's POWER service. Missing months are skipped, never counted as zero.

01

Remove the seasons

Every January minus the average January, and so on. What's left shows the long-term drift without the summer–winter swing.

02

Fit a straight line

Its slope is the change per decade; slope × record length is the total change.

03

Measure the uncertainty

A 95% range for the rate and a t-test p-value. Below 0.05 we call it significant.

04

Correct for memory

A warm month is often followed by another, so 500 months hold less information than it seems. We account for that.

05

Get a second opinion

The seasonal Mann-Kendall test checks for a steady rise or fall without assuming a bell curve.

Note

Monthly data from 1981 for NASA POWER measurements (sunlight and clouds from 1984, haze from 2001) and from 1998–2004 for the satellite and land-model records (GPM, GLDAS, MODIS, OMI, AIRS), averaged over 1° squares. MERRA-2 blends satellite and ground observations with a weather model, so values describe a grid square, not a single weather station. Trends are straight-line summaries. They show what changed, not why. Map: OpenFreeMap, © OpenMapTiles, © OpenStreetMap contributors; outline Natural Earth.
