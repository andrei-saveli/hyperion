Did London’s emissions zone reduce NO₂?

On 8 April 2019, central London began charging drivers of vehicles that fell below its emissions standards. Use roadside measurements to test what happened after the zone began.

Make a prediction

How will central London’s NO₂ change compared with an estimate of what would have happened without the zone?

Check the comparison before the policy

Researchers adjusted the weekly roadside records for weather. ByteMe builds a comparison from roadside monitors outside London and fits their weights using the early part of the record. The final eight pre-policy weeks test how well that comparison predicts London before the charge begins.

Test what changed after launch

Check two rival explanations

  • London was already cleaner than its comparison. Try the adjustment to see how much of the later gap remains if the earlier gap would have continued.
  • One monitor drove the finding. Remove the largest donor to see whether the result depends on it.

A causal interpretation depends on the comparison tracking what London would have done without the zone. Earlier changes in vehicle fleets or other London-specific changes can affect the estimate.

Method

How we decide if a change is real

Simple, transparent statistics, the same for every place and measurement. A straight line is a simplification, and we say so.

00

Read the NASA record

Monthly values since 1981 for the grid square (about 50 km wide) around your place, from NASA's POWER service. Missing months are skipped, never counted as zero.

01

Remove the seasons

Every January minus the average January, and so on. What's left shows the long-term drift without the summer–winter swing.

02

Fit a straight line

Its slope is the change per decade; slope × record length is the total change.

03

Measure the uncertainty

A 95% range for the rate and a t-test p-value. Below 0.05 we call it significant.

04

Correct for memory

A warm month is often followed by another, so 500 months hold less information than it seems. We account for that.

05

Get a second opinion

The seasonal Mann-Kendall test checks for a steady rise or fall without assuming a bell curve.

Note

Monthly data from 1981 for NASA POWER measurements (sunlight and clouds from 1984, haze from 2001) and from 1998–2004 for the satellite and land-model records (GPM, GLDAS, MODIS, OMI, AIRS), averaged over 1° squares. MERRA-2 blends satellite and ground observations with a weather model, so values describe a grid square, not a single weather station. Trend cards summarize recorded changes.
